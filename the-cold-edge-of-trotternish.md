The rain over Trotternish did not fall so much as hang, suspended in the salt air like a damp shroud.

DCI Ewan MacLeod stood at the lip of the basalt cliff, his collar turned up against the Atlantic wind. Below him, the sea battered the jagged black rocks of the Quiraing coast with a rhythmic, thunderous violence. It was six in the morning, but on the Isle of Skye in late October, dawn was little more than a slight pale bruising on the eastern horizon.

"Not much left to pick over, Boss," Sergeant Mairi Campbell said, her voice barely carrying over the gales. She gestured down toward a narrow ledge resting fifty feet above the boiling surf.

Half-submerged in a tangle of wet kelp lay a battered leather trunk, its brass fittings rusted green by salt. It hadn't washed ashore; the sheer drop of the cliffs made that impossible. It had been thrown from the height above.

MacLeod knelt near the edge, his boots sinking into the saturated peat. He reached down to pick up a single, waterlogged object that had snagged on a clump of gorse just inches from the drop: a silver pocket watch, its crystal cracked, its hands frozen forever at 3:14.

"Any sign of the car?" MacLeod asked, scanning the single-track road that wound away into the mist toward Staffin.

"No skid marks. No oil," Mairi replied, pulling her notepad close to her chest to protect it from the deluge. "Whoever brought it out here knew the headland. And they knew the tides."

MacLeod wiped a mixture of rain and sea spray from his eyes, staring out at the grey expanse of the Minch. Skye had a way of keeping its secrets buried in the peat or drowned in the sea. But as the coastguard winch began its slow, whining descent toward the ledge below, he knew the island was about to give one up.
